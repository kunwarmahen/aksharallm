Pictures — turning a picture into integers, and back

A language model only ever sees integers. To make it draw, a picture first has to become a short list of integers that can be turned back into the picture. That is the image tokenizer: an encoder shrinks a 64×64 picture to a small grid, each cell is replaced by the number of its nearest entry in a learned codebook, and a decoder paints the picture again from those numbers. It is the audio codec of chapter 21 in two dimensions, with the same quantizer. Read chapter 26.

picture64×64×3 → encoder3 halvings → codebooks8×8 grid × 4 numbers → decoder3 doublings → picture again

Does the picture still say the same thing?

Pixel error (PSNR, higher is better) is the usual number and a weak one: most of a shapes picture is background, so a picture can lose a whole shape and keep a good PSNR. So every reconstruction is also read back by a program that counts the blobs, names their colour and tells a square from a circle from a triangle. It is right on every original (the first row), so whatever it gets wrong is the tokenizer's fault. Each row uses more codebooks: more numbers per picture, a closer copy. The held-out pictures are purple triangles, a combination the training pictures never contain.

The pictures themselves

Each row: the original, then the reconstruction from 1, 2 and 4 codebooks.

Two tokenizers, picture by picture

The 8×8 grid spends 256 numbers on a picture; the 16×16 grid spends 1,024. Paired on the same pictures: how many does one read right where the other reads wrong, and is the difference more than chance (p, an exact sign test)? 


    


    

      
From a sentence to a picture

      

        The second step (G1): a small transformer, the same kind that writes
        text, reads a caption such as two red circles and writes the picture's code
        numbers one at a time; the tokenizer's decoder paints them. Nothing here is copied from
        a training picture: it is drawn fresh each time, and the reader checks it.
        Guidance pushes the drawing towards the caption (1 = none; 3 is the
        usual setting): higher obeys the words more and varies less.
        Several kinds of model can draw here: G1 writes the code numbers one at
        a time, G2 starts from pure noise and clears the whole picture in 50
        small steps (flow matching, the way Stable Diffusion 3 works), G3 does
        that in the tokenizer's smaller latent space, and G4 reads a whole
        sentence with our own frozen language model, so you can type it in your own words.
        Purple triangles are never shown in training, though both words are,
        with other partners: drawing one means the model learned what the words mean, not
        which pictures go with which caption.

Every caption, judged

      

        A report draws several pictures for each caption the words can make (60 for shapes, 270
        for scenes, which also say size and place) and reads every one back. With a contrastive
        model chosen under Run it from here, the report also says what our learned judge thinks
        of every picture, and how often it agrees with the reader. Copies counts drawings identical, code for
        code, to a training picture (memorising rather than drawing). Variety
        is the share of a caption's pictures that differ from each other.

A judge that learned (G4b)

      

        Every score above comes from the rule-based reader, which only works because we drew the
        pictures ourselves. Real image models are judged by a contrastive model
        that has learned which captions go with which pictures. This is ours, trained here on
        our own pictures and captions: a picture counts as right when, of every caption its
        corpus can say, the one it was drawn for is the closest. It is trusted only where it
        agrees with the reader (kappa 1 = always, 0 = no better than chance),
        which is the point of checking it on pictures whose facts are known. It is our own
        feature space, so this is not CLIPScore and not FID.
      

      

        Scenes are the second corpus: captions that also say size and place
        (“two big red circles on the left”), with two combinations never drawn in training:
        purple triangles, and circles on the right.

From a sentence to a photo (G4c)

      

        COCO 2017: 118,287 everyday photos, each cut to a 64×64 square, each with five sentences
        written by people. A model draws one picture for each validation caption. Nothing can
        read a photo back by rule, so every number comes from our contrastive model trained on
        COCO, and each is shown beside the same number for the real photo of
        that caption (as good as it gets) and beside chance.
        Top-5: of all the captions, is the drawing's own one of the five that
        fit it best? Distance compares the drawings with the real photos as a
        whole, in our model's features (the formula FID uses, not FID itself); two halves of
        the real photos give the distance that means “indistinguishable”.
